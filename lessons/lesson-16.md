
# ACS 4310 - Wrap-up, Review & What's Next

## Overview

You've built three visualizations. Before the final assessment, take stock: do you actually have everything the rubric asks for? What do your charts reveal that the raw data didn't? How would you make them better? And once this class ends, where does this skill take you?

<!-- > -->

## Why you should know this

Data visualization isn't "draw a chart" — it's "find something true in a pile of numbers and show it to someone else clearly." That skill doesn't end with this course. Reviewing your own work critically, explaining what it reveals, and knowing how to keep improving are the habits that make you better at this long after the class is over.

## Review your work (self-audit)

Pull up your three visualizations and go through this checklist for **each one**. Don't just eyeball it — open the code and the rubric side by side.

- [ ] Does it answer **three questions** about the dataset?
- [ ] Does it display, compare, and contrast **at least three data points/fields** from the dataset?
- [ ] Do you understand your dataset well enough to explain where it came from and what each field means?
- [ ] Does it run with **no console errors**?
- [ ] Is it submitted (repo link, or clearly separated folder) and posted to GradeScope?

Re-read the [Assignment 2 rubric](../Assignments/assignment-2.md#evaluating-your-work) row by row. For each visualization, which column are you actually in — *Does not meet*, *Meets*, or *Exceeds*? Be honest; this is the same rubric your final assessment will be judged against.

<!-- > -->

## What is data visualization, and what did yours reveal?

In one or two sentences, without looking anything up: **what is data visualization, and why does it matter?**

Now get specific. For each of your three visualizations, finish this sentence: *"Before I built this, I didn't know ___. After looking at it, I can see ___."*

If you can't finish that sentence for one of your charts, that chart is decoration, not visualization — it's not revealing anything you couldn't already see in a spreadsheet. That's worth noticing now, not after it's graded.

<!-- > -->

## Peer review: how can you improve?

Trade with a partner. Spend 5 minutes on each other's work using **only** the rubric categories — Dataset, Details, Story, Technique, Code quality. For each one, give one specific, actionable note (not "looks good" — something they could actually go change).

Common, fast improvements worth checking for:

- Missing or unclear axis labels, legend, or units
- A color scheme that doesn't match the data (e.g. sequential color for categorical data)
- A chart answering only one question when it could answer two or three with better encoding
- Noisy data with no trend line to show the underlying direction (see [lesson-14](./lesson-14.md))
- No interaction (tooltip, hover, filter) where one would make the data easier to explore (see [lesson-15](./lesson-15.md))

Pick at least one note you received and fix it before you submit.

## Show & tell

In pairs or small groups: present **one** of your three visualizations like you're explaining it to a stakeholder who's never seen the data. What question does it answer? What's the one-sentence takeaway? Thirty seconds, no slides needed — just you and the chart on screen.

## Where can you go next?

This course covered the fundamentals — scales, axes, shapes, D3 selections, Canvas, SVG. The field goes a lot further:

- **Build a portfolio piece.** Clean up your best visualization, write a short README explaining the dataset and what it reveals, and pin it on GitHub. This is one of the more effective, low-effort portfolio pieces you can have for interviews.
- **Go deeper with D3.** Hierarchies (treemaps, pack layouts), real geographic maps, force-directed graphs, and animated transitions are all one step beyond what you built here. [D3 in Depth](https://www.d3indepth.com) and [Observable](https://observablehq.com) are the best places to keep going.
- **Try a different tool.** [Observable Plot](https://observablehq.com/plot/) and [Vega-Lite](https://vega.github.io/vega-lite/) trade some of D3's flexibility for speed — worth knowing when a project doesn't need D3's full power.
- **Follow working examples.** The [New York Times graphics desk](https://www.nytimes.com/spotlight/graphics) and [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org) publish real, professional data visualizations constantly — a good habit is to occasionally ask "how would I build that?"
- **Keep the job-skill angle in mind.** Dashboards, internal reporting tools, and marketing/product analytics all lean on exactly what you practiced here.
